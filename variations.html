Mexican Train Dominoes Variations: 2-Player, Chickenfoot, Double-9 & More

Once the official rules feel comfortable, these rule twists keep the table fresh — and every one of them can be practiced in the online game. They change the pace, the player count, and the strategy — and every variation below can be practiced for free in the online game by adjusting the settings.

How to play Mexican Train Dominoes with two players

Head-to-head Mexican Train is a genuinely tactical duel — shrink the set and make two small adjustments:

  • Drop to a double-nine set (55 dominoes) so rounds stay brisk — ten dominoes apiece is the norm.
  • Both players still build from the same engine (the highest double held), and the Mexican Train works exactly the same.
  • With only one rival, an open train is deadly — protecting your personal train matters twice as much.
  • Play the traditional three rounds, or race to a score limit: first to 100 points loses.

In the online game, choose 1 opponent and any set size — the computer adjusts the deal automatically.

Is Mexican Train the same as Chickenfoot? What's the difference?

Chickenfoot and Mexican Train are cousins — both are "build trains from a central double" games — but they feel very different at the table:

Mexican TrainChickenfoot
TrainsOne personal train per player + one shared Mexican TrainPersonal trains plus "chicken feet" branching off every double
DoublesMust be covered with one matching dominoMust be covered by three dominoes, creating the "chicken foot"
EngineHighest double (or a fixed double per round)Usually the highest double, placed in the center
FeelInteractive: everyone shares the Mexican TrainMore explosive: every double forks the layout

To play Mexican Train with a chickenfoot twist, use this house rule: when a double is covered, the covering player must also play a second tile branching off the double, opening a mini-train anyone may extend.

Playing with different domino sets

  • Double-6 (28 dominoes): a lightning-quick version for 2–4 players — 7 dominoes each, and with four players there's no boneyard at all, so passing is common and blocking strategy rules.
  • Double-9 (55 dominoes): the sweet spot for two or three players.
  • Double-12 (91 dominoes): the classic set for up to four players — this is the default in the online game.
  • Double-15 (136 dominoes): for five or six players at the table, or a marathon two-hour four-player game. Hand sizes stay at 15 or fewer.
  • Double-18 (190 dominoes): for big groups. One round can take an hour — great for family reunions, less great for a lunch break.

Popular house rules

  • Fixed engine rounds: instead of the highest double, round one uses 12|12, round two 11|11, and so on — a full game is thirteen rounds.
  • No drawing: if you can't play, your train opens and you pass immediately. Faster, and meaner.
  • Open doubles: a covered double stays playable as a branch, giving the table extra endpoints to build on.
  • Partners: four players pair up, sit opposite each other, and add their scores together. Lowest team total wins.
  • Zero bonus: going out earns you a 5-point bonus subtracted from your score — rewards fast hands.

Which variation should you try first?

If you usually play the classic four-player double-12 game, try two-player double-9 for a tactical duel, or the no-drawing house rule to shake up your group's habits. All the set sizes are built into the online game — pick them in settings and play.
